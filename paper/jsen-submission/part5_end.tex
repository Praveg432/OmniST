\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Each guarantee has a narrow scope.} Theorem~\ref{thm:coverage} is an average over which sensor is
new and needs errors from the evaluated window, so it cannot be run at forecast time. Theorem~\ref{thm:aci} holds
for each sensor at forecast time but only for its long-run average: it certifies 0.81 to 0.89 per sensor on the
traffic networks, is weak or vacuous with the 6 to 18 windows of the daily networks, and says nothing about a
single window. Neither gives coverage conditional on location.
\item \textbf{Delhi is not covered at forecast time}: 0.855 to 0.877 with six evaluation windows, and no
procedure here certifies it.
\item \textbf{Infinite intervals.} Per-sensor ACI issues an infinite interval in 2\% to 8\% of windows
($\gamma=0.1$), 40\% on the 6-sensor Beijing set, and more with $\gamma=0.5$.
\item \textbf{Few calibration sensors are expensive}: a finite same-window 90\% interval needs at least 9.
\item \textbf{The learned forecaster is not the best simple one everywhere.} Seasonal-naive forecasting
beats OmniST on PeMS-BAY (MAE 3.56 against 4.68, intervals 24\% narrower), persistence ties it on weather
and PeMSD7-M, and coordinates and attention add no measured accuracy. The training budget was fixed.
\item \textbf{Not evaluated}: Generalized HCP, HCP's second-moment variant, sequential whitening, and other
inductive forecasters under the forecasting task.
\item \textbf{Data and history.} Targets include imputed values; traffic positions are reconstructed from
distance matrices; architecture settings come from earlier Delhi development, so (A3) holds only up to that
history; a sensor with no history of its own is not handled; the largest network has 325 sensors.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
OmniST forecasts at sensors it never trained on and keeps working when reference sensors are removed. The more
durable result is about calibration. With neighbors drawn from the training network, a new sensor's error is a
function of its own data and its neighbors alone (Lemma~\ref{lem:locality}). That makes two existing guarantees
apply to a forecaster on a changing network, with different scope. Counted in calibration sensors, conformal
calibration covers a randomly drawn new sensor in expectation (Theorem~\ref{thm:coverage}); on five networks it
held in every finite case, at a width cost that falls from several-fold to about 10\% as calibration sensors
grow from 10 to 90, but it calibrates on the evaluated window. Run per sensor, adaptive conformal inference covers
every sensor at forecast time in the long run (Theorem~\ref{thm:aci}); it lifted the worst sensor from 0.58 to
0.89 on PeMS-BAY, certified 0.81 to 0.85 per sensor on the traffic networks, and covered the farthest sensors as
well as the rest, at the price of occasional infinite intervals. The usual pooled recipe, by contrast, averages
near target and leaves individual sensors badly covered. Coordinates and attention, in our implementation, add no
measured accuracy over a shared per-sensor model. Open problems are a finite-sample guarantee at forecast time
for short histories, coverage conditional on a sensor's location, and a comparison with Generalized HCP and
other inductive forecasters.
